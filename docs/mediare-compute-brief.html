Mediare conducts fully online extrajudicial mediations

A committee of AI models analyzes each case by consensus — and qualified human mediators supervise and formalize every settlement.

Public dataset · 500 cases GenLayer Intelligent Contract São Paulo state court rulings Product mockup →
In short

Mediare is a working demonstration that GenLayer's consensus protocol can decide something harder than a price feed: the merits of a real legal dispute. A panel of AI readings interprets the same Brazilian civil case under deliberately different evidentiary standards — disagreeing about how strictly to weigh the proof, never about which side to favour — and independent validators must agree on the consolidated result before the chain accepts it.

The first round of testing is done, against real rulings from the São Paulo state court, and it shows the approach is viable. The consensus layer is stable — identical results reproduce across independent deployments — and the panel's money range contains the court's own figure on roughly six cases in ten. The same tests show precisely where the system still fails, and we publish that too.

US$ 500 in LLM credits is what we need from the GenLayer Foundation to tune and test the Intelligent Contract systematically, instead of one variant at a time on a personal card. Section 05 sets out exactly what that buys, and what a larger second phase would add.

01What we are building

Brazil's Law 13.140/2015 makes extrajudicial mediation binding: a signed settlement is an enforceable instrument under CPC art. 784 III, with the same force as a court judgment. The bottleneck is not the law, it is preparation. A mediator needs to walk into the room already knowing what the documents support, what is genuinely contested, and what number range a court would find defensible.

Mediare produces exactly that document. Three lenses read the same case file and disagree by evidentiary method — strict, broad, and precedent-driven — not by which party they favour. A deterministic Python step consolidates them; no language model performs arithmetic or applies the final classification rule. The whole committee is one file — ic.py, about 400 lines, prompts and consolidation included. The result is a range, a majority position, and a named dissent.

Why a blockchain

The panel runs as a GenLayer Intelligent Contract. Five independent validators re-execute it and must agree under an equivalence principle before the result is accepted. A mediation agenda that both parties will rely on should not rest on one model's single sample — and the disagreement itself is recorded, permanently, for anyone to audit.

What the committee actually hands over

The panel's second output block is a draft settlement in Brazilian legal form, written from the range the lenses agreed on. It is the document the mediator opens the session with — a starting position both parties can react to, not a decision imposed on them. The human mediator is not removed from the loop. The contract's output is an agenda, not a settlement — the mediator reads it, runs the session, and signs. A mockup of the platform shows where that agenda lands for the parties and the mediator.

175 cases derive from real first-instance rulings of the São Paulo state court (TJSP), 319 are synthetic, 6 are hand-audited gold. Categories span the disputes that actually reach mediation in Brazil: neighbour damage, condominium, traffic, tenancy deposits, used-vehicle sales, renovation contracts, consumer goods.

The labels were the real work

The first labelling pass produced 530 distinct free-text value keys — danos_materiais_deferidos, valor_pedido_A_B_S_S, total_condenacao_periodo. A heuristic could trust only 44% of them, and the exclusions were not random. We rebuilt every label from the original ruling into a closed four-rubric vocabulary, after stripping party names, court numbers and addresses from the source text.

Follow one dispute end to end

Case 0005 is a renovation contract: a terrace leaked, the ceiling and flooring were ruined, and the court found fault on both sides. It is the case that took us four contract versions to get right, and the three files below are everything the system sees and is judged against.

FileWhat it is
casos/0005.json The input: each party's documents and pleading, as a mediator would receive them. No ruling, no outcome.
gabaritos/0005.json The label: what the court actually decided — shared fault, and R$ 32,367.44, exactly half of the R$ 64,734.88 in proven damage.
ic.py The committee: three lenses, the equivalence principle validators must agree on, and the deterministic consolidation.

Early versions answered R$ 64,734.88 — the full damage, ignoring the split they had themselves concluded. Version 8 answers R$ 32,367.44, and five independent validators reproduced it.

03How the contract evolved

Nine versions. The instructive ones are where a fix in one place exposed a defect somewhere else.

VerChangeWhat it taught
v4Consolidate on the total, not rubric by rubricTwo lenses reaching the same sum by different routes agree. Rubric placement is noise — in the labels too.
v5–v7Successive edits to the equivalence principleIterating against a single on-chain transaction is not evidence. Each version was contradicted by the next run.
v8Apportionment must reach the numberA panel concluding "shared fault" while reporting the full amount is incoherent. Fixed a case we had chased for four versions.
v9Tolerant JSON parsing; no LLM arithmeticThe contract was crashing on unescaped quotes. Because the crash was deterministic, all validators reproduced it and the network returned MAJORITY_AGREE — a failure sold as consensus.
The finding that changed our method

Our off-chain evaluation harness had its own JSON parser, so it measured code that was not the code running on-chain. Clean off-chain, broken on-chain, and we were reading the off-chain number. Harness and contract now execute literally the same functions, with only the model call injected.

We also ran a redesign that failed: splitting the panel into a merits stage and a valuation stage made results worse in both directions. Asked in the abstract whether an obligation exists, the lenses affirmed it 43% of the time versus 26% when forced to write a number. Quantification, it turns out, is the discipline that produces denial. The dead end is documented in the repository rather than deleted.

The first two numbers are the product working: on roughly two thirds of cases a mediator would see a range that both contains the defensible answer and is tight enough to anchor a conversation. On-chain, 13 of 16 Studio transactions reached valid consensus, and identical ranges were reproduced across independent deployments — the consensus layer is stable.

The last two are the honest problem, and they are why we are asking for compute rather than declaring victory.

  • The committee does not know how to say no. Where the court awarded nothing, the panel still proposes money in 54% of cases, and in nine of them it does so unanimously, with a range of zero width. Three of those errors reproduce to the cent across two different models and four prompt versions — they are systematic, not sampling noise.
  • Unanimity carries no information. Unanimous panels are right 58% of the time; divergent panels, 66%. We designed an auto-settlement path on the premise that agreement signals correctness. The data killed that premise, and the product now routes everything through a human.

05What compute unlocks

Everything above was funded out of pocket, and the shape of the work shows it: samples sized to what a personal card could absorb, variants tried one at a time instead of swept systematically, and a single measurement pass over 128 cases that had to be restarted twice when the credit ran out mid-run. Validator inference on GenLayer's test network is free; the evaluation that makes those runs worth starting is not. That budget bought a working system and an honest map of its failures. It does not stretch to fixing them.

The economics are unusually legible here, because every experiment has a known unit cost:

Systematic tuning against the reference sample

Nine contract versions so far, almost all of them one variant at a time against whatever sample was to hand. That is how we ended up with changes contradicted by the next run.

  • A sweep of roughly 20 prompt and equivalence-principle variants against all 128 reference cases, each with confidence intervals — about US$ 130, and it replaces guesswork with a ranking.
  • The model ladder: Haiku, Sonnet and Opus as panel members and as validators, to find the cheapest configuration that holds quality. A mediation platform for Brazilian small claims cannot run at frontier pricing per case. About US$ 60, or 11 million tokens.
  • Replication runs, so we can finally state how much of the measured error is the committee and how much is the ruler disagreeing with itself.
What phase 1 cannot settleAt 128 cases, only differences of roughly twenty points are distinguishable from noise. A real ten-point improvement — which is what we expect from good tuning — would look like nothing. Phase 1 produces strong candidates, not proof.

Ten times the data, aimed at the failure we cannot currently see

Court rulings are public and free to collect, so this is not about access to disputes. Every case has to be labelled from its ruling and then measured, and at 1,500 cases one evaluation pass costs around US$ 80, or 14 million tokens — and a study is never one pass.

  • The dataset goes from 175 real cases to roughly 1,500, and — the number that matters — from 48 cases where the court awarded nothing to roughly 400. The denial gap rests on those 48 today, which is why its confidence interval still spans twenty-seven points.
  • The phase 1 winner is re-measured at a sample size where a ten-point gain is actually detectable. Our power analysis puts that threshold near 1,000 clean cases.
  • On-chain validation at scale. Validator inference on GenLayer is free but each transaction takes 5 to 40 minutes; every credit spent off-chain is what makes those slow runs worth starting.
Why it has to be secondPhase 2 measures a specific configuration very well. Running it first would mean spending the larger budget proving out whichever variant we happened to guess — exactly the mistake this brief documents us making at small scale.
Either way, it stays open

The dataset, the evaluation scripts, the contract and the measurements are public and will remain so, including the results that do not flatter the system. The two-stage panel that failed is in the repository with its numbers attached, and so is every ruler bug we found in our own measurements.
